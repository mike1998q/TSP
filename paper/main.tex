\begin{abstract}
Long-term multivariate forecasting must combine local time-domain dynamics
with global periodic structure compact in the frequency domain. Mamba
forecasters model one view but not both, and deep models often discard the
simple linear maps that remain near state-of-the-art. We present \model{}, a dual-domain \emph{framework} whose
time branch (decomposition with a selective state-space encoder) and
frequency branch (learned spectral filtering) each produce a forecast, fused
by a convex gate. Two principles organize it: \emph{linear anchoring} ---
each branch carries a DLinear/FITS-style linear pathway with zero-initialized
nonlinear heads, so at initialization the model is a linear map of the
instance-normalized window; and \emph{capacity placement} --- a bidirectional
Mamba over the \emph{variate} dimension is switched on per dataset. We further
separate normalization from dispersion \emph{modeling}: RevIN is a reversible
window-level normalization, not a scale forecast, so an optional dispersion
head predicts a positive per-horizon scale from multi-resolution history,
generalizing de-normalization toward explicit time-varying scale. On nine
benchmarks under the look-back-96 protocol (three seeds), \model{} reports
the lowest mean MSE on seven, improving over reported S-Mamba on five (up to
17\% on Solar) and tying three. Paired ablations validate capacity placement
in both directions (the variate mixer helps weather yet hurts ETTh1;
normalization helps weather yet hurt solar); data diagnostics show the
datasets' scale is non-stationary yet predictable, motivating the dispersion
head. Dual-domain modeling is a per-dataset structural option, not a
universal gain.
\keywords{Time series forecasting \and State-space models \and Mamba \and
Frequency domain \and Multivariate analysis}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Long-term forecasting of multivariate time series underpins applications
from power-grid load planning to traffic management and weather-dependent
operations. The field has cycled through architectural paradigms at a
remarkable pace: Transformer variants with efficient attention
\cite{informer,autoformer,fedformer}, patch-based attention
\cite{patchtst}, inverted variate-token attention \cite{itransformer},
and, most recently, selective state-space models (SSMs) built on Mamba
\cite{mamba,smamba}, which offer linear-time sequence modeling with
input-dependent dynamics.

\emph{Three empirical gaps} complicate this progress. \textbf{Gap~1: deep
models discard strong linear maps.} A per-component linear projection from
the look-back window to the horizon (DLinear \cite{dlinear}) and a tiny
complex-linear interpolation of the low-passed spectrum (FITS \cite{fits})
match or beat far larger models on several benchmarks, especially the ETT
family; deep architectures that ignore this spend their early training
re-discovering a linear map, and on small datasets often never do.
\textbf{Gap~2: the useful inductive bias differs by domain.} Local trends
and regime changes are naturally expressed in the time domain, while
multi-scale seasonality is compact in the frequency domain, yet models
committed to a single view --- attention or SSMs over time steps only, or
spectral mixing only \cite{frets} --- systematically underuse the other.
\textbf{Gap~3: the right amount of capacity is dataset-dependent.}
Cross-variate mixing is the decisive component on high-dimensional
benchmarks \cite{itransformer,smamba} yet pure overfitting capacity on
low-channel data, so any \emph{fixed} architecture is mis-sized for one
regime or the other.

We answer these three gaps with \model{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), mapping
each to one structural remedy: \emph{(i)}~an explicit
linear anchor with zero-initialized nonlinear corrections in each branch;
\emph{(ii)}~parallel time and frequency branches, fused by a convex gate
that preserves both prediction paths; and \emph{(iii)}~a bidirectional
variate mixer switched on or off per dataset. Each remedy is detailed
below.

Concretely: \emph{(i)~linear anchors} --- each branch carries a
DLinear-style \cite{dlinear} or FITS-style \cite{fits} linear pathway with
zero-initialized nonlinear corrections, so at initialization the model
reduces to a linear map of the instance-normalized window (the RLinear-class
structure \cite{rlinear,revin}), a controlled test finding this
accuracy-neutral; \emph{(ii)~dual branches} whose forecasts form a learned
convex combination gated on both features, so each domain keeps an explicit
path (ablations find the frequency branch significant on Solar and within
noise elsewhere --- per-dataset, not universal); and \emph{(iii)~capacity
placement} --- a causal Mamba over time and a bidirectional Mamba over
variates, switched per dataset (removing the mixer hurts weather while adding
it hurts ETTh1, each significant). Beyond these, an optional
\emph{dispersion head} generalizes RevIN de-normalization to \emph{forecast}
a positive per-horizon scale (Sect.~\ref{sec:dispersion}), moving
normalization from preprocessing toward explicit time-varying-scale modeling.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} \textbf{(1)}~A dual-domain architecture
whose output is a gated convex combination of per-branch forecasts;
\textbf{(2)}~a linear-anchoring scheme reducing to a linear map at
initialization (accuracy-neutral in a controlled test);
\textbf{(3)}~a capacity-placement recipe for selective SSMs;
\textbf{(4)}~a dispersion head that forecasts a per-horizon scale, with data
diagnostics showing the scale is non-stationary yet predictable; and
\textbf{(5)}~the lowest reported average MSE on seven of nine benchmarks
(three-seed mean$\pm$std), with a released harness and paired ablations
including negative, dataset-dependent findings.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Transformer forecasters.} Informer \cite{informer}, Autoformer
\cite{autoformer} and FEDformer \cite{fedformer} adapt attention to long
sequences via sparsity, decomposition, and frequency mixing;
Non-stationary Transformers
\cite{nonstationary} restore the stationarity that normalization removes,
and Crossformer \cite{crossformer} attends across both time and variates.
PatchTST \cite{patchtst} treats sub-series patches as tokens with channel
independence; iTransformer \cite{itransformer} inverts the axes and attends
over \emph{variates}, establishing that cross-channel modeling drives
accuracy on high-dimensional benchmarks. \model{} adopts the variate-mixing
insight with linear-time bidirectional SSMs, coupled to an explicit
frequency branch and linear anchors.

\paragraph{Linear and frequency-domain models.} DLinear/NLinear
\cite{dlinear} showed decomposition plus a linear map rivals Transformers;
RLinear \cite{rlinear} added reversible instance normalization \cite{revin},
and the MLP-based TiDE \cite{tide} and TimeMixer \cite{timemixer} confirm
that lightweight mixers stay competitive. In the spectrum, FreTS \cite{frets} learns
frequency-domain MLPs and FITS \cite{fits} forecasts with a single
complex-linear interpolation of the low-passed spectrum at negligible cost. \model{} does not compete with
these models --- it \emph{contains} them: each branch carries a linear
pathway of its domain's family, with the deep machinery trained as a
correction.

\paragraph{State-space models.} Mamba \cite{mamba} introduced selective SSMs
with input-dependent dynamics and linear-time scans, extended by Mamba-2
\cite{mamba2}. In forecasting, S-Mamba \cite{smamba} and TimeMachine
\cite{timemachine} apply bidirectional Mamba over variate tokens, and further
works explore frequency-domain SSMs \cite{fmamba}. \model{} unifies these threads:
causal Mamba over time, bidirectional Mamba over frequency bins and
variates, placed per dataset rather than uniformly.

\paragraph{Decomposition and heteroscedasticity.} Splitting a series into
interpretable components is a century-old idea: classical additive models,
STL \cite{stl}, wavelet analysis, EMD, and SSA extract a trend, a seasonal
part, and an irregular remainder, and learned decompositions such as SCINet
\cite{scinet} carry it into deep forecasters. A
recurring limitation, made explicit by the seasonal-trend-\emph{dispersion}
decomposition (STD) \cite{std}, is that these methods model \emph{level} but
not \emph{scale}: when a series' variance changes over time ---
heteroscedasticity --- the fluctuation is left in the irregular term. We
relate to this lineage at two clearly separated levels. \emph{Diagnosis:}
RevIN \cite{revin} performs reversible, window-level normalization by the
\emph{input} mean and standard deviation; it is emphatically \emph{not} a
dispersion decomposition and does not \emph{forecast} future scale, but by
removing per-window scale it exposes whether a dataset's apparent
nonstationarity is genuine scale shift (which it absorbs) or periodic
structure (which it distorts) --- a distinction Sect.~\ref{sec:dispersion}
measures directly. \emph{Modeling:} an optional dispersion head
(Sect.~\ref{sec:dispersion}) predicts a positive per-horizon, per-variate
scale $\hat{\mathbf{D}}$ from multi-resolution history and reconstructs
$\hat{\mathbf{Y}}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}+\hat{\mathbf{D}}\odot\hat{\mathbf{S}}$, a
genuine ``trend plus dispersion times seasonal shape'' in the spirit of STD
--- though we \emph{predict} scale rather than \emph{extract} it, and make
no claim of producing an STD/STDR remainder. The seasonal-trend split
itself lands on the \emph{linear anchors} (Sect.~\ref{sec:anchors}). The
same discipline of routing a specific deficiency to a dedicated operator has
been argued for frequency-domain design in adjacent fields \cite{dfir}.

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Normalization}

Given a look-back window $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times C}$ of $L$
steps and $C$ variates, the task is to predict the next $H$ steps
$\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times C}$. Following RevIN \cite{revin}, we
standardize each window per variate, $\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = (\mathbf{X} -
\boldsymbol{\mu}) / \boldsymbol{\sigma}$, and de-normalize the final
forecast. Instance normalization is treated as a per-dataset switch rather
than a universal default; Sect.~\ref{sec:ablation} shows why.

\subsection{Time Branch: Decomposition, Linear Backbone, Selective Scan}
\label{sec:anchors}

The time branch decomposes $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$ with a moving average into
trend $\mathbf{X}_t$ and seasonal $\mathbf{X}_s$ components
\cite{autoformer,dlinear} and forecasts with two cooperating paths.

\emph{Linear backbone.} Per-component linear maps project the window
directly to the horizon:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \mathbf{W}_s \mathbf{X}_s +
\mathbf{W}_t \mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times C},
\qquad \mathbf{W}_s, \mathbf{W}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times L},
\end{equation}
i.e., a DLinear-style map \cite{dlinear} (randomly initialized and
trained jointly --- a parameterized pathway, not a fitted DLinear),
shared across variates.

\emph{Selective-scan correction.} The pair $[\mathbf{X}_s; \mathbf{X}_t]$
is embedded per time step and processed channel-independently by a stack
of Mamba layers \cite{mamba} (Fig.~\ref{fig:blocks}a). A Mamba layer
discretizes a state-space system with input-dependent parameters
$(\boldsymbol{\Delta}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C})$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_k = e^{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_k \mathbf{A}}\,\mathbf{h}_{k-1} +
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_k \mathbf{B}_k\, u_k, \qquad
y_k = \mathbf{C}_k^{\top} \mathbf{h}_k + D\, u_k ,
\label{eq:ssm}
\end{equation}
scanned causally over the $L$ steps and applied independently to each of
the $d_{\mathrm{in}}$ feature dimensions: per dimension, the state is
$\mathbf{h}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$, $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ is
diagonal, the input $u_k$ is scalar, and
$(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_k \in \mathbb{R},\; \mathbf{B}_k, \mathbf{C}_k \in
\mathbb{R}^{N})$ are produced from $u$ by learned linear maps
\cite{mamba}. The last state, summed with a
whole-window linear embedding $\mathbf{W}_e \tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(c)}$
(one per variate, as in \cite{smamba}), forms the branch feature
$\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{time}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d}$. A head
$\phi_{\mathrm{time}}$ maps features to a horizon correction:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{time}} = \mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{lin}} +
\phi_{\mathrm{time}}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{time}}), \qquad
\phi_{\mathrm{time}} \equiv \mathbf{0} \text{ at init.}
\label{eq:timezero}
\end{equation}
Because $\phi_{\mathrm{time}}$ is zero-initialized, the branch's output at
initialization equals its (randomly initialized) DLinear-style backbone;
the SSM learns only what the linear map cannot express. We run the
recurrence in fp32 even under mixed precision, as the $L$-step
accumulation overflows in half precision and silently destroys training.

\subsection{Frequency Branch: Spectral Filtering and a FITS Anchor}
\label{sec:freq}

The frequency branch applies the real FFT along time,
$\mathbf{Z} = \mathrm{rFFT}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}) \in
\mathbb{C}^{C \times F}$, $F = \lfloor L/2 \rfloor + 1$, optionally
low-passed by zeroing the top fraction $\rho$ of bins.

\emph{Spectral encoder.} A learnable complex linear filter
$\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}_r + i\mathbf{W}_i \in \mathbb{C}^{F \times F}$
densely mixes bins, $\mathbf{Z}' = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}^{\top}$ along the
frequency axis (a bidirectional Mamba over the bin sequence is an
input-dependent alternative). The (real, imaginary) parts are projected to
the branch feature $\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{freq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d}$
and a head $\phi_{\mathrm{freq}}$ produces the spectral forecast.

\emph{FITS anchor (optional, per dataset).} Mirroring the time branch, a
complex linear map
$\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{C}^{F' \times F}$ with
$F' = \lfloor (L{+}H)/2 \rfloor + 1$, applied along the frequency axis
($\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{U}^{\top} \in \mathbb{C}^{C \times F'}$), maps the
(low-passed) window spectrum to that of a length-$(L{+}H)$ series, whose inverse transform's last $H$
samples are the branch's linear spectral forecast:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{freq}} =
\underbrace{\mathrm{irFFT}_{L+H}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{U}^{\top})[\,L{:}\,]}_{\text{linear-in-frequency}}
\; + \; \phi_{\mathrm{freq}}(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{freq}}),
\qquad \mathbf{U}, \phi_{\mathrm{freq}} \equiv \mathbf{0} \text{ at init.}
\label{eq:fits}
\end{equation}
This pathway \emph{parameterizes} the FITS \cite{fits} family, but unlike
FITS itself $\mathbf{U}$ is \emph{zero-initialized}, so the frequency branch
is silent at initialization (the length-$L$ and length-$(L{+}H)$ frequency
grids do not align, so no canonical identity init exists, and a random
$\mathbf{U}$ would inject noise during the highest-learning-rate epochs).
With the time head, spectral map, and gate all zero or constant at init, the
end-to-end map in the normalized space is the linear
$g_0\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{lin}}$; composed with RevIN this is the
RLinear-class structure \cite{rlinear,revin} --- linear in the normalized
window, by construction not fitted. The anchor is enabled per dataset
(Table~\ref{tab:hparams}); where disabled, linear anchoring rests on the
time branch alone.

\subsection{Cross-Channel Mixing over Variates}
\label{sec:mixer}

Channel independence is a strong baseline \cite{patchtst,dlinear} but
leaves accuracy on the table when variates are coupled
\cite{itransformer,smamba}. Inside each branch, before its head, we
optionally run $M$ layers of \emph{bidirectional} Mamba over the $C$
per-channel features, followed by a position-wise feed-forward block
(Fig.~\ref{fig:blocks}b). Bidirectionality is essential: channel order
carries no causal direction, so each layer sums a forward and a reversed
scan in one residual stream. The mixer is a per-dataset choice: $M{=}2$ at
width 512 on electricity/traffic (its single most load-bearing component;
Sect.~\ref{sec:ablation}), $M{=}2$ at width 256 on weather, $M{=}1$ on
solar, and $M{=}0$ on the few-channel ETT and exchange datasets, where it
triples parameters without adding signal.

\subsection{Forecast-Level Convex Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

Each branch emits a forecast and a feature. The model output is a gated
convex combination
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y} = g \odot \mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{time}} + (1 - g) \odot
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{freq}}, \qquad
g = \sigma\!\big(\psi([\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{time}};
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{freq}}])\big) \in (0,1)^{C\times 1},
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
with $\psi$ applied row-wise (per variate), followed by RevIN
de-normalization. Since $\mathbf{Y}$ lies elementwise between the branch
forecasts, both domains retain an explicit prediction path, and the gate
value reports each branch's convex mixing weight --- a lightweight proxy
for its contribution, not a causal attribution. The constraint is
structural, not behavioral: $g$ is a sigmoid and can saturate near 0 or 1,
so one branch may contribute very little --- by design, as the ablations
show the useful domain is dataset-dependent. $\psi$ is zero-initialized
with bias $2.2$, so training starts at $g \approx 0.9$: the time branch is
linear at initialization while the frequency branch is silent, and an even
$0.5/0.5$ mix would waste the highest-learning-rate epochs on noise.

\subsection{Dispersion Head: Forecasting Time-Varying Scale}
\label{sec:dispersion}

RevIN reconstructs a forecast as $\mathbf{Y}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}
\odot\mathbf{S}$ with the input-window mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and standard
deviation $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ held \emph{constant} over the horizon --- a
reversible normalization, not a forecast of how the scale evolves. We
optionally generalize the reconstruction to
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \hat{\mathbf{T}} + \hat{\mathbf{D}}\odot\mathbf{S},\quad
\hat{\mathbf{D}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\mathrm{softplus}\big(\psi_D(\mathbf{s})\big)
\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H}_{>0},\quad
\hat{\mathbf{T}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\psi_T(\mathbf{s}),
\label{eq:dispersion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{S}$ is the normalized-space fused forecast (the shape) and
$\hat{\mathbf{D}}$ is a \emph{positive, per-horizon, per-variate} scale ---
unlike RevIN's single $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ per variate. The heads
$\psi_D,\psi_T$ are zero-initialized so that $\hat{\mathbf{D}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}$
and $\hat{\mathbf{T}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}$ at start: the module \emph{begins}
exactly as RevIN de-normalization and learns per-horizon corrections, keeping
$\hat{\mathbf{D}}$ anchored to the input scale and strictly positive. This is
STD's ``trend plus dispersion times seasonal shape'' with the scale
\emph{predicted}, not extracted. \emph{Multi-resolution history:} the heads
read $\mathbf{s}$, the per-variate mean and std of the past
$\{96,144,288,336\}$ samples ending at the forecast origin (only pre-origin
information), so the scale predictor sees cycles longer than the $L{=}96$
look-back --- Solar's $144$-step daily and Traffic's weekly periods --- without
enlarging the branch inputs. A ``fixed'' variant instead sets
$\hat{\mathbf{D}}$ to the longest-resolution historical std (non-learned).

\subsection{Training}
We minimize MSE with AdamW under a halving schedule
($\eta_e = \eta_0 \cdot 2^{-(e-1)}$), which concentrates learning in the
first epochs and suppresses the late-epoch overfitting that cosine
schedules invite on small benchmarks; all runs use 10 epochs with
best-checkpoint early stopping.

\subsubsection{Implementation and Reproducibility.}
\label{sec:repro}
All experiments use PyTorch 2.11 (CUDA 13.0) on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090.
Optimization is AdamW (grad clip $5.0$), 10 epochs with the learning rate
halved each epoch, early stopping on validation loss, and mixed precision

decomposition kernel is 25; Mamba blocks use expansion 2 and conv width 4
throughout. Table~\ref{tab:hparams} lists all per-dataset settings; configs
and the ablation harness ship with the code. All main results are means
over \emph{three seeds} (2024--2026); the maximum per-entry std across the
36 dataset--horizon entries is $0.005$ MSE, typically $0.001$--$0.003$.
\paragraph{Statistical methodology.} We compare at two levels.
\emph{Within our framework} (ablations), every variant shares seeds with
the reference, so we report \emph{paired} per-seed differences with
$t$-based 95\% CIs ($n{=}3$) and treat a component as supported only when
its CI excludes zero. \emph{Against quoted baselines}, pairing is
impossible, so we use a conservative unpaired tie threshold of $0.005$
MSE. Our in-framework DLinear re-run shows why: through the identical
pipeline on ETTh1 it averages $0.462$/$0.456$ MSE/MAE versus its quoted
$0.456$/$0.452$ --- a $+0.006$ implementation gap, the order of several
margins in Table~\ref{tab:main}. Cross-paper comparisons therefore cannot
support sub-$0.01$ distinctions; \model{}'s margin over the in-framework
DLinear ($0.439$ vs.\ $0.462$) is, by contrast, a same-pipeline comparison.

\paragraph{Dispersion diagnostics: is the time-varying scale real?} We test
the interpretation directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:dispersion}; no model training).
Standardizing each channel and taking a rolling std over the dominant period,
all three probed datasets carry substantial time-varying scale (coefficient
of variation $0.20$--$0.48$); a KPSS test rejects stationarity of the
rolling-std series in \emph{every} sampled channel, and a Ljung-Box test
finds it strongly autocorrelated --- the scale is non-stationary and
\emph{predictable}, exactly what a dispersion head can exploit. Yet the
benefit of \emph{normalizing} by it is dataset-dependent: removing RevIN
helps solar ($-0.029$ avg MSE) but hurts traffic ($+0.100$). The distinction
is structural, not statistical --- solar's night zeros are \emph{periodic
structure} (window statistics encode the night fraction, and
de-normalization amplifies errors by an unstable std), whereas traffic
windows carry genuine distribution shift (weekday/weekend, per-sensor levels)
that normalization absorbs. We therefore ship normalization as a verified
per-dataset switch, and position the dispersion head (Sect.~\ref{sec:dispersion})
as the route to \emph{forecast} the predictable component of the scale rather
than only normalize by its input estimate.

\paragraph{Dispersion head: evaluation protocol.} We evaluate the head with a
four-variant chain under shared seeds, splits, and budget --- base (no
normalization), then RevIN, fixed historical dispersion, and the learned head
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:dispersion}) --- reporting, besides MSE/MAE, the error on
high- vs.\ low-variability windows and, to match the interpretability
standard of \cite{std}, ADF/KPSS/Ljung-Box statistics of the residual
\emph{before and after} the head (does explicit scale prediction remove
heteroscedasticity that would otherwise stay in the error?). The head,
multi-resolution statistics, and this chain ship in the released harness; a
same-pipeline probe confirms it trains stably and reduces to RevIN at
initialization. As the diagnostics predict a dataset-dependent outcome, a
head that helps only high-variability datasets would still support the
per-dataset placement claim. The full chain with the residual battery, on the
RTX~5090, is reported in the extended version.

\paragraph{Negative results that shaped the design.} Deepening the traffic
mixer from two to four layers ($19.7\rightarrow37.9$M) gave no gain, so the
release keeps two; enabling the mixer on ETT ($8.4$K training windows)
tripled parameters and \emph{caused} premature early stopping through
overfitting, so the released ETT configurations disable it.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{Where each domain wins.} On solar the branch-only ablations
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}) show both domains
contribute but the time view carries more (removing it costs $+0.012$ vs.\
$+0.002$ MSE) --- consistent with the look-back hiding periodic structure
(the 10-minute daily cycle spans $144{>}L{=}96$ samples). The fusion gate
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}) exposes per-sample convex weights as a lightweight
proxy: on a trained ETTh1 model the gate settles below equal mix, agreeing
with the ablation (though only the ablation is causal).

\paragraph{Efficiency profile.} Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} reports
per-dataset measurements that are hardware-independent. Parameter count is
governed
by model \emph{width}, not channel count (electricity and traffic share
$19.7$M despite a $2.7{\times}$ gap in variates), so model size is decoupled
from data dimensionality; compute and activation memory instead scale with
the channel count through the variate mixer (traffic $60$\,GMACs vs.\
electricity's $22$ at equal width, while ETT and exchange sit two orders of
magnitude below), making the capacity restraint there cheap as well as
accurate. Wall-clock figures are left for the extended version.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\model{} is a dual-domain state-space forecasting \emph{framework}: each
branch carries a linear pathway, deep components are zero-initialized
corrections, and capacity (especially cross-variate mixing) is placed per
dataset. Under the look-back-96 protocol it achieves the lowest reported
average MSE on seven of nine benchmarks at $0.09$--$19.7$M parameters. The
paired ablations validate capacity placement in \emph{both} directions (the
variate mixer helps weather yet hurts ETTh1; normalization helps weather yet
hurt solar), find the frequency branch significant on Solar, and find zero
initialization accuracy-neutral. Separately, we sharpen the relation to
dispersion decomposition: RevIN normalizes but does not \emph{forecast}
scale, so an optional dispersion head predicts a per-horizon scale from
multi-resolution history --- with diagnostics confirming the scale is
non-stationary yet predictable. We thus claim per-dataset structural
flexibility, not a universal gain, plus a caution: normalization helps or
hurts depending on whether apparent nonstationarity is genuine shift or
periodic structure.

Limitations, each with its resolving experiment: (1)~the frequency-branch
gain is shown on one dataset ($n{=}3$); (2)~zero initialization's stability
value is untested; (3)~baselines are quoted from \cite{smamba}, not re-run
(the $+0.006$ DLinear gap, Sect.~\ref{sec:repro}); and (4)~the dispersion
head's full four-variant chain across datasets, with the residual-stationarity
battery, is reported in the extended version. The fixed 96-step look-back
hides long cycles (traffic's weekly) that the multi-resolution dispersion
statistics begin to address.
